# Cause and Effect Essay

> **At a glance.** Explains why something happened, or what resulted from it, by demonstrating the causal mechanism rather than asserting a link.

A subtype of [Expository Essay](./expository.md).

## Components

In the order they typically appear:

### Thesis Statement (required)

States the essay's central claim up front so the reader knows, before reading further, what the whole essay resolves — the crux the rest of the essay answers.

**For this type:** States the causal claim being made — which cause(s), which effect(s).

> "The library's new self-checkout system cuts wait times but removes a point of human contact many patrons relied on."

### Causal Link (required)

Shows the mechanism connecting a cause to its effect, so the claim is a demonstrated link rather than an assumed one.

**Note:** The component most responsible for catching a correlation mistaken for causation.

> "Shorter queues mean fewer patrons leave before checking out at all — that's the direct mechanism behind the circulation increase, not just correlation with the season."

### Transition (required)

Signals the logical relationship between two ideas (sequence, contrast, cause) so the reader doesn't have to infer it.

> "In contrast, the point-of-sale data tells a different story."

### Conclusion (required)

Leaves the reader with why the essay mattered, not just a repeat of what it said.

**Note:** A weak conclusion restates the thesis verbatim; a working one synthesizes or states significance.

> "Faster checkout is a real gain — but a library that measures only speed will miss what it's trading away."

## Organizational patterns

Named ways to arrange this type's components, chosen by the writer:

### Single Cause, Multiple Effects

One cause is shown producing several distinct effects.

### Multiple Causes, Single Effect

Several independent causes are shown converging on one effect.

### Causal Chain

A leads to B leads to C — each link is a cause of the next.

## Pitfalls

- Correlation presented as causation with no mechanism shown.
- A single-cause narrative for a phenomenon that's actually multi-causal.
- A causal chain with a missing link the reader has to infer.
