# Components

> **At a glance.** A component is an atomic building block an essay is assembled from — a
> paragraph-or-smaller unit with one job. Essay types don't redefine these; they compose
> them, in an order, marking each required or optional for that type.

## Hook

Earns the first few seconds of the reader's attention before the essay has proven it's worth reading.

**Placement:** opening

Optional wherever the opening component itself already does this job (dominant-impression in descriptive, baseline-definition in definition essays).

> "The library's self-checkout machines process a book in four seconds. The librarian they replaced also asked how your week was going."

## Orienting Details

Gives the reader the who/what/when/where they need before the sequence of events can make sense.

**Placement:** opening

> "The summer I turned twelve, my grandfather taught me to fillet a fish on the dock behind his house."

## Thesis Statement

States the essay's central claim up front so the reader knows, before reading further, what the whole essay resolves — the crux the rest of the essay answers.

**Placement:** opening

What counts as a valid thesis shifts by type: a neutral topic claim in expository writing, an arguable position in persuasive writing, an interpretive claim in literary analysis. See each essay type's note on this component.

> "The library's new self-checkout system cuts wait times but removes a point of human contact many patrons relied on."

## Topic Sentence

States the one idea a body paragraph exists to support, so a reader can track the essay's argument paragraph by paragraph without reading every sentence.

**Placement:** body

> "The first reason self-checkout saves time is that it removes the queue for a single staffed register."

## Evidence

Backs a claim with something outside the writer's own say-so — a fact, statistic, example, or expert source — so the reader has a reason to believe it beyond the writer's word.

**Placement:** body

In expository writing this stays factual/neutral; in persuasive writing it directly supports a reason for the claim; in literary analysis this specializes into textual-evidence.

> "Average checkout time dropped from 90 seconds to 12 seconds after the machines were installed (library annual report, 2025)."

## Textual Evidence

Supports an interpretive claim about a text using the text's own words, so the interpretation is checkable against the source rather than asserted.

**Placement:** body

> "'He could not remember when the house had last been quiet' signals the isolation the rest of the chapter develops."

## Close Reading

Explains how a piece of textual evidence actually supports the claim — the step that turns a quote into an argument instead of decoration.

**Placement:** body

> "The word 'remember' implies the quiet is not new but has become invisible to him — which is the isolation the essay's thesis names."

## Transition

Signals the logical relationship between two ideas (sequence, contrast, cause) so the reader doesn't have to infer it.

**Placement:** any

> "In contrast, the point-of-sale data tells a different story."

## Conclusion

Leaves the reader with why the essay mattered, not just a repeat of what it said.

**Placement:** closing

A weak conclusion restates the thesis verbatim; a working one synthesizes or states significance.

> "Faster checkout is a real gain — but a library that measures only speed will miss what it's trading away."

## Plot Sequence

Carries the reader through what happened, in the order it happened, so the story is followable.

**Placement:** body

> "He handed me the knife handle-first, then knelt beside me to show the angle."

## Conflict and Climax

Gives the story a stake worth reading for — something at risk — and the moment it comes to a head.

**Placement:** body

> "The blade slipped and I dropped it — and for a second I was sure he'd send me back inside."

## Resolution

Shows how the conflict actually ended, so the reader isn't left mid-scene.

**Placement:** body

> "He picked the knife up, wiped it, and put it back in my hand. 'Again,' he said."

## Reflection

States what the events meant to the writer — the 'so what' that turns a sequence of events into a story with a point.

**Placement:** closing

The component most narrative drafts skip, leaving a sequence of events with no stated point.

> "I didn't learn to fillet a fish that day so much as learn what it looks like to be trusted with something sharp."

## Dominant Impression

Names the single overall feeling or idea every detail in the essay will serve, so the details add up to one picture instead of a list.

**Placement:** opening

> "The workshop smelled like a place where things got fixed, not made."

## Sensory Detail

Gives the reader something to see, hear, smell, taste, or touch, so the description is experienced rather than told.

**Placement:** body

> "Sawdust caught the light from the one high window and never seemed to fully settle."

## Figurative Language

Uses comparison (simile, metaphor) to convey a quality that a literal description would only state.

**Placement:** body

> "The vise sat at the bench's edge like a hand permanently closed."

## Counterargument

States the strongest version of the opposing view, so the essay's rebuttal actually earns the reader's agreement instead of dodging the hard case.

**Placement:** body

A strawman counterargument (a weak version of the opposing case) is the most common failure of this component.

> "Opponents point out that self-checkout eliminates jobs for the library's part-time staff."

## Rebuttal

Answers the counterargument directly, showing why the essay's claim survives it.

**Placement:** body

> "But the same budget that funded the machines also funded two new community-programming positions — the jobs moved, they didn't disappear."

## Call to Action

Tells the reader what to do with the position they've just been persuaded of.

**Placement:** closing

> "Ask your branch what it did with the labor savings before you decide whether the tradeoff was worth it."

## Comparison Points

States the same basis of comparison applied to each subject, so the comparison is apples-to-apples instead of a list of unrelated facts about each.

**Placement:** body

> "On cost: self-checkout runs $0.02/transaction in maintenance; a staffed register runs $0.85 in wages."

## Causal Link

Shows the mechanism connecting a cause to its effect, so the claim is a demonstrated link rather than an assumed one.

**Placement:** body

The component most responsible for catching a correlation mistaken for causation.

> "Shorter queues mean fewer patrons leave before checking out at all — that's the direct mechanism behind the circulation increase, not just correlation with the season."

## Materials or Prerequisites

Tells the reader what they need before starting, so they don't discover a missing requirement mid-process.

**Placement:** opening

> "You'll need a library card and the branch's mobile app installed before your first self-checkout visit."

## Sequential Steps

Walks the reader through the process in the order it must be performed, so no step depends on one that hasn't happened yet.

**Placement:** body

> "First, scan your card. Then scan each book's barcode. Finally, tap 'finish' before removing your books from the pad."

## Tips and Warnings

Flags the mistakes or edge cases a first-timer would otherwise hit and have to recover from.

**Placement:** body

> "If the machine doesn't beep, the scan didn't register — check the screen before moving to the next book."

## Baseline Definition

Gives the reader a shared starting point for the term before the essay extends or complicates it.

**Placement:** opening

> "A 'third place,' as sociologist Ray Oldenburg defined it, is a space that is neither home nor work."

## Extended Definition

Fills out the baseline definition with what the term looks like in practice, what it isn't, and what it resembles, so the reader can recognize it beyond the dictionary line.

**Placement:** body

> "A library is a third place; a self-checkout kiosk with no staff nearby to talk to is closer to a vending machine that happens to dispense books."
